## The big idea: inoculation / prebunking

Showing someone a weakened dose of a manipulation technique *before* they meet it
in the wild builds resistance, the same way a vaccine does. Two moves:
- **Forewarn** — name the technique and that it's coming.
- **Refute pre-emptively** — show the trick and why it works.
A good "vaccine" is short (4–5 sentences), memorable, and generalizable to *any*
future topic, not just this one claim.

## Lateral reading (the core habit)

Amateurs read *vertically* — they stay on the page and judge it by how polished
it looks. Professional fact-checkers read *laterally*:

> **STOP** (don't read deeply yet) → **LEAVE THE PAGE** (open new tabs) →
> **CHECK WHAT INDEPENDENT SOURCES SAY** about this site/author →
> **FIND BETTER COVERAGE** (search the claim itself, not just this page).

Key insight: you cannot evaluate a source from *inside* the source. A
professional-looking site proves nothing. Check what *others* say about it.

## The 5 source-credibility questions

Ask these of ANY source. If the answer is "I don't know," apply proportional
skepticism.
1. **WHO** is behind this — a named organization, or anonymous?
2. **WHAT** is their track record — flagged by fact-checkers? Wikipedia view?
3. **DO they cite evidence** — primary sources, named experts, specific data?
4. **WHO ELSE** reports this — only this site, or reputable outlets too?
5. **WHAT is the purpose** — inform, persuade, sell, or generate outrage?

## Cognitive biases the red flags exploit

| Bias | How it's weaponized | Your defense |
|---|---|---|
| Affect heuristic | Strong emotion short-circuits scrutiny | Notice the feeling; pause before sharing |
| Confirmation bias | Content flatters what you already believe | Actively search the opposing case |
| Authority bias | A "Dr." / "expert" with no relevant field | Check the *specific* credential vs. the claim |
| Availability heuristic | One vivid anecdote feels like a trend | Ask for base rates / systematic data |
| Truth-by-repetition | Seeing it often feels like evidence | Frequency ≠ accuracy; trace to origin |
| In-group/tribal bias | "Us vs. them" framing lowers your guard | Judge the claim, not the team it serves |
| Proportionality bias | Big events "must" have big hidden causes | Mundane explanations are usually right |

## Defense habit per red-flag category

- **A — Emotional manipulation:** when content makes you *feel* before you
  *think* (fear, outrage, urgency to share "before it's deleted"), that urgency
  is the tell. Slow down; verify before forwarding.
- **B — Source/attribution:** no named author or no citations = treat as a rumor
  until corroborated by a Tier 1–4 source. "Sources say" is not a source.
- **C — Logical fallacies:** restate the argument plainly. If the conclusion
  doesn't follow once the emotion and labels are stripped, the logic is the
  product, not the evidence.
- **D — Temporal/contextual:** check the *date* and the *original context* of
  any photo, quote, or study. Old/real material in a new/false frame is the most
  common manipulation. Reverse-image-search images.
- **E — Coordinated/structural:** identical wording across many accounts,
  clickbait headline vs. thin body, or "everyone is saying" with no named who —
  signs of amplification, not consensus.
- **F — Health-specific:** "miracle cure", "they don't want you to know",
  "all-natural so it's safe", or a raw adverse-event report cited as proof of
  harm. Reporting ≠ causation. Check Cochrane/PubMed and WHO/EMA/CDC; prefer
  scientific *consensus* over "a study found."

## Domain quick-guides

**Health:** PubMed and Cochrane for systematic evidence; WHO/EMA/CDC/national
authorities for guidance. Hierarchy: systematic review > RCT > peer-reviewed
study > preprint > blog. Adverse-event databases are open-submission *signals*,
not proof of causation. "A study found X" ≠ "the scientific consensus is X."

**Political / geopolitical:** verify legislative or policy claims against the
primary text (official gazettes, EUR-Lex, government sites), not a summary.
Identify state-controlled media. Separate news reporting from editorial/opinion.
Be extra cautious on emotionally charged, identity-coded topics.

## One-line vaccines (drop-in)

- *Emotional bait:* "If a post wants you angry before it wants you informed,
  it's selling the anger."
- *Fake authority:* "A title isn't a credential — match the expertise to the
  exact claim."
- *Cherry-picking:* "One data point is an anecdote; ask what the full series
  shows."
- *Clickbait:* "If the headline and the body disagree, believe the body — or
  neither."
- *Conspiracy logic:* "An explanation that can never be wrong is never an
  explanation."
- *Old-content recycling:* "Before you share, check the date and the first
  appearance."
